1. Dung beetles (Scarabaeinae) -- multiple groups' work

This is probably your best bet. Scarabaeinae are the single most well-inventoried insect group across Amazonian sites, with standardized pitfall-trap protocols that make cross-site comparison straightforward. Key sources:

Larsen, Lopera, & Forsyth and collaborators have done intensive dung beetle inventories across Peruvian Amazon sites (Tambopata, Manu, etc.) with species-level identifications. Larsen's RAP (Rapid Assessment Program) surveys for Conservation International include Amazonian sites in Peru, Suriname, and elsewhere, with species x site occurrence matrices often in supplementary materials or RAP bulletins.
Salomão et al. (2022) and the broader PPBio/RAPELD network (ppbio.inpa.gov.br) -- 
ppbio.inpa.gov.br
the PPBio program uses standardized RAPELD modules across multiple Amazonian sites to inventory taxa including beetles, frogs, and vegetation. PPBio has a data portal where some datasets are downloadable. Dung beetle data from multiple RAPELD modules in the Brazilian Amazon (Ducke Reserve near Manaus, sites along BR-319, Viruá, etc.) may be available there.
Gardner, Barlow, Peres et al. have published extensively on dung beetles across sites in eastern Amazonia (particularly the Bragança region and the Tapajós), comparing forest, logged, and pasture sites. Their datasets often include full species x site matrices.
2. Fruit-feeding butterflies (Nymphalidae)

www.researchgate.net
While trap-sampling only fruit-feeding butterflies using a standardized protocol provides comparable data across multiple sites, this guild has been surveyed extensively. Key sources:

DeVries & Walla (2001) and DeVries et al. (2012) developed and applied standardized bait-trap protocols. 
besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com
A major strength of standardized sampling is that it allows robust comparisons of diversity in space and time, and their study assessed species diversity across 60 consecutive months of simultaneous sampling. Their Ecuadorian Amazon (Jatun Sacha, Yasuní) and Costa Rica sites have published species-abundance data.
Graça et al. and other INPA-associated researchers have published fruit-feeding butterfly inventories from multiple Central Amazon sites using standardized protocols. Some of this work ties into the PPBio network.
3. Ants (Formicidae)

Longino & Colwell (1997) pioneered structured inventory approaches. In Amazonia specifically, look at work from the TEAM (Tropical Ecology Assessment and Monitoring) Network -- they use standardized Winkler and pitfall protocols for ground-dwelling ants across multiple tropical sites including Amazonian ones (Manaus, Caxiuanã, Yasuni). TEAM data may be downloadable from their portal (teamnetwork.org).
The PPBio network also has ant inventory data from multiple RAPELD modules.
Other possibilities worth checking
TEAM Network (teamnetwork.org) -- standardized protocols for multiple taxa (including ground-dwelling arthropods via Winkler litter extraction) across tropical sites. Several Amazon sites are included. Data is sometimes available through their portal or Wildlife Insights.
Conservation International RAP bulletins -- these rapid biological assessments often include insect inventories (especially dung beetles, ants, sometimes Lepidoptera) with species lists from multiple sites within a survey area. Check the RAP Bulletin of Biological Assessment series; many are freely available as PDFs.
GBIF -- you could query GBIF for targeted datasets from specific Amazonian research programs, filtering by dataset rather than individual records, which might help identify well-sampled survey datasets vs. scattered museum records.
Practical recommendation
If I had to pick one group most likely to give you what you need quickly, it's dung beetles. The taxonomy is relatively well-resolved for the Neotropics (thanks largely to Vaz-de-Mello and colleagues), the sampling protocol is standardized and exhaustive (baited pitfall traps), and there are more comparable multi-site Amazonian datasets for this group than probably any other insect taxon. Larsen's work in particular often includes complete species x site abundance matrices.
